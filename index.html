The peptide guide

Nine peptides, sorted by where they stand today.

  • ApprovedLicensed as a medicine for specific uses. Needs a prescription.
  • In trialsBeing tested in humans. Not yet approved.
  • Not approvedNo licensed human use. Mostly animal or lab data.

Semaglutide

Ozempic, Wegovy, Rybelsus

Approved
What it is
A GLP-1 receptor agonist. It copies a gut hormone that lowers blood sugar, slows digestion, and reduces appetite. Approved for type 2 diabetes and, as Wegovy, for weight management.
Evidence
Strong. Large randomised human trials (the STEP and SUSTAIN programmes).
Common side effects
Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, constipation. Listed warnings include pancreatitis and gallbladder problems.
Where to get it
Only with a doctor's prescription from a licensed pharmacy.

Sources

  • STEP 1 trial, Wilding et al., NEJM 2021 (PubMed)
  • Official US drug labels (DailyMed)

Tirzepatide

Mounjaro, Zepbound

Approved
What it is
A dual GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist. It acts on two gut hormones instead of one. Approved for type 2 diabetes and weight management.
Evidence
Strong. Large randomised human trials (the SURPASS and SURMOUNT programmes).
Common side effects
Similar to semaglutide: mainly stomach and gut effects.
Where to get it
Only with a doctor's prescription from a licensed pharmacy.

Sources

  • SURMOUNT-1 trial, Jastreboff et al., NEJM 2022 (PubMed)
  • Official US drug labels (DailyMed)

Retatrutide

LY3437943 (Eli Lilly)

In trials
What it is
A 'triple agonist': it acts on GLP-1, GIP and glucagon receptors. Early trials showed large weight loss. As of September 2026 it is still investigational and not approved anywhere.
Evidence
Promising but incomplete. Phase 2 published; phase 3 results still coming in.
Safety
Long-term safety is not yet known. Online 'retatrutide' is not the real drug — the only legitimate supply is inside clinical trials.
Where to get it
Nowhere legally, outside of an official clinical trial.

Sources

  • Phase 2 trial, Jastreboff et al., NEJM 2023 (PubMed)
  • Registered trials (ClinicalTrials.gov)

Tesamorelin

Egrifta

Approved
What it is
A growth-hormone-releasing hormone (GHRH) analogue. Approved in the US for one narrow use: reducing excess belly fat in people with HIV-associated lipodystrophy.
Evidence
Good for its approved use. Not proven for general fat loss or muscle gain.
Sport
Prohibited by WADA (growth hormone releasing factors).
Where to get it
Prescription only, for its approved use.

Sources

  • Official US drug label (DailyMed)
  • Clinical trials (PubMed)

Bremelanotide (PT-141)

Vyleesi

Approved
What it is
A melanocortin receptor agonist. Approved in the US for low sexual desire (HSDD) in premenopausal women. Online "PT-141" vials are not the approved product.
Evidence
Moderate, for its approved use (the RECONNECT trials).
Common side effects
Nausea, flushing, headache, temporary rise in blood pressure.
Where to get it
Prescription only, for its approved use.

Sources

  • Official US drug label (DailyMed)
  • RECONNECT trials (PubMed)

BPC-157

"Body Protection Compound" — 15 amino acids

Not approved
What it is
Marketed for tendon, gut and injury healing. Most data comes from rats and lab studies. There are no large randomised human trials.
Evidence
Weak in humans. Only small, uncontrolled reports.
Regulation
In 2023 the US FDA put it in Category 2 — substances that raise significant safety concerns for compounding (immunogenicity, impurities, too little human safety data).
Sport
Banned by WADA.
Where to get it
No licensed source. "Research use only" vials are unregulated and not meant for humans.

Sources

  • FDA: bulk substances with safety risks
  • Review of BPC-157 evidence and regulation (PMC)

TB-500

Synthetic fragment of thymosin beta-4

Not approved
What it is
Marketed for recovery and tissue repair. Thymosin beta-4 has been studied in animals and in early human research for wound and eye healing, but TB-500 as sold online is not a studied medicine.
Evidence
Mostly animal and lab data.
Sport
Prohibited by WADA.
Where to get it
No licensed source.

Sources

  • Thymosin beta-4 research (PubMed)
  • WADA Prohibited List

CJC-1295 & Ipamorelin

Growth hormone secretagogues, often sold as a pair

Not approved
What it is
Both push the body to release more growth hormone. CJC-1295 is a GHRH analogue; ipamorelin mimics ghrelin. Neither is approved as a medicine.
Evidence
Limited. A few small early human studies; no long-term safety data.
Sport
Prohibited by WADA.
Where to get it
No licensed source.

Sources

  • CJC-1295 research (PubMed)
  • Ipamorelin research (PubMed)
  • WADA Prohibited List

GHK-Cu

Copper peptide

Not approved as a drug
What it is
A small copper-binding peptide found naturally in the body. Widely used in topical skincare products. Injectable versions are not an approved medicine.
Evidence
Some lab and small skin studies for topical use. Little human data for injection.
Where to get it
As an ingredient in regular cosmetic skincare.

Sources

  • GHK-Cu skin research (PubMed)

Before you buy anything online

Most peptides sold online are labelled 'research use only'. Nobody checks their purity, dose, or sterility, and fakes of popular drugs are common. We won't point you to any seller we can't verify.

For approved medicines, the safe route is a doctor and a licensed pharmacy. In Nepal, medicines are regulated by the Department of Drug Administration (DDA).

This page is for education only. It is not medical advice. Talk to a qualified doctor before using any peptide. Competitive athletes: many of these are banned in tested sport.

Why testing matters 🧪 A label is just a sticker. Testing shows what's actually inside. Key tests for peptides: 🔬 HPLC: checks PURITY (how much is the real peptide?) ⚖️ Mass spectrometry: checks IDENTITY (is it the right molecule?) 🦠 Endotoxin test: checks for bacterial toxins 💧 Sterility test: checks for live germs 📄 A Certificate of Analysis (COA) is a report of these results. But ask: ✔️ Was it tested by an independent lab, or by the seller? ✔️ Does the batch number match the product? ✔️ Is the lab name real and checkable? No proper testing = no way to know what you're getting. ⚠️ Education only. Not medical advice.

"It worked in rats!" 🐀 So why doesn't that mean it works in you? Animal studies are an important first step. But humans are not big rats. Different bodies, doses, metabolism, and lifespans mean results often don't carry over. 📊 About 90% of drug candidates that reach human clinical trials fail, mostly because they don't work well enough or aren't safe enough. So when a peptide has only animal data, the honest answer is: "Promising, but unproven in humans." Source: Sun et al., 2022, Acta Pharmaceutica Sinica B: "Why 90% of clinical drug development fails and how to improve it?" ⚠️ Education only. Not medical advice.

How to read a study in 60 seconds ⏱️ Before you believe any "new study says..." post, check these 6 things: 1️⃣ WHO was studied? Humans, animals, or cells in a dish? 2️⃣ HOW MANY? 10 people is weak. 1,000+ is strong. 3️⃣ Was there a CONTROL group? Without one, you can't compare. 4️⃣ HOW LONG? 4 weeks tells you little about long-term safety. 5️⃣ WHO PAID for it? Check the funding section. 6️⃣ Is it PEER-REVIEWED? Published in a real journal, not just a website? Save this post and use it every time 📌 ⚠️ Education only. Not medical advice.
